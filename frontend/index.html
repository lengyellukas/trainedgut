The science

Your gut
adapts
to what you feed it

Past two hours of racing, carbohydrate availability — not fitness — is what starts to fail first. Feed the gut more carbs regularly and it builds the transporters to absorb them.

93% Ultra-endurance athletes report GI symptoms during their events. Gut training is now listed by sports-dietetics bodies as an intervention with consistent beneficial outcomes.
60% Total GI symptoms reduced by gut training across a systematic review of eight trials - with carbohydrate malabsorption down 45-54% and gut discomfort down 47%.
28% Less carbohydrate needed for the same fuel oxidation when feeding is personalised - with lower stomach fullness and lower perceived effort.

Every claim below links to the paper it came from. Four studies, all published since 2017, all in endurance athletes.

Sports Medicine · Costa et al., 2025 · SDA / Ultra Sports Science Foundation position statement

Up to 93% of ultra-endurance athletes report GI symptoms during their events. Two sports-dietetics bodies now list gut training among strategies with consistent beneficial outcomes.

Sports Medicine · Martinez et al., 2023 · Systematic review

Across eight trials of gut training: gut discomfort down 47%, carbohydrate malabsorption down 45-54%, total GI symptoms down 60-63%. Protocols used carbohydrate gels every 20 minutes at 90 grams per hour.

European Journal of Applied Physiology · Podlogar et al., 2022

At very high hourly intake, shifting the glucose-fructose ratio from the classic 2:1 toward 1:1 raised how much fuel athletes actually absorbed - with no difference in GI comfort.

Performance Nutrition · Podlogar et al., 2025

Personalised carbohydrate feeding delivered the same fuel oxidation on 28% less carbohydrate, with lower stomach fullness and lower perceived effort in trained athletes.

What the evidence doesn't say

Two weeks of very high carbohydrate plus gut training produced no extra performance benefit in elite race-walkers, and slightly more GI symptoms (King et al., 2022). A 2025 review argues the evidence for ≥100 g/h improving performance is still limited (Wilson). We think the failure mode in both cases is "more carbs for everyone" - the opposite of a progressive, individual protocol. But you should know the argument exists.

Energy gels exist. Training plans exist. A program that teaches your gut to handle race-day fuel - that's what's been missing.

The fair question

Couldn't I just
do this myself?

You can. The rules are public. Start slightly below your current tolerance, add up to 10 grams per hour every two or three weeks, hold when a session goes rough, shift the glucose-to-fructose ratio as the dose climbs, and don't try anything new in the last two weeks.

The catch is holding that line for sixteen weeks while your training changes, the race gets closer, and every long day is a chance to overshoot and set yourself back a fortnight. The protocol isn't information. It's the week-by-week structure that keeps the progression honest.

Is this for you

This is for you if

  • You race longer than 90 minutes
  • You've had a race, or a long training day, where your stomach ended it
  • You suspect you're underfuelling, and every attempt to eat more has backfired
  • You already train seriously, and you have a race in the calendar that matters
  • You'd rather follow a plan than improvise on the day

This isn't for you if

  • Your events are under 90 minutes
  • Your race is less than 8 weeks away
  • You have a diagnosed gastrointestinal condition

If you're on the right and want to be on the left, the honest answer is wait for the next race in your calendar. The protocol needs weeks, not days.

Scope and safety

TrainedGut is a sports nutrition training protocol. It is not medical advice and it is not designed to diagnose or treat any condition.

Persistent or severe gastrointestinal symptoms deserve a doctor, not a training plan. If symptoms continue outside exercise, involve blood, or are getting worse, see one.

If you have a diagnosed gastrointestinal condition, are pregnant, or manage diabetes or another condition affected by carbohydrate intake, talk to a medical professional before starting.

Progressive carbohydrate loading is well tolerated in the published research, but individual response varies. Nothing here is a guarantee of performance.
